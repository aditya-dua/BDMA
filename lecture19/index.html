The Deliverable

One sentence. Four load-bearing words.

Take a real dataset, model it in a non-relational database, build a dashboard, and present a recommendation the data supports.

Each of those four words is a place marks are lost.

WordDemandsCommon failure
RealData someone generated by doing something200 synthetic rows with no missing values
ModelA deliberate shape decisionA CSV dumped into one flat collection
DashboardA view a named person opens on MondayCharts the tool offered
RecommendationAn action, an owner, a number"Worth looking into further"
Teams of 3–4. Split by stage — data, model, queries, narrative — not by chart. Everyone speaks.
Step 1 · Dataset

Bring your own. Please.

You may use bdma.rides. You already know its answers: 62.29% completion, 17.99% driver cancellations, ₹13,33,539 estimated leak, 18:00 the busiest hour at 1,373 bookings, and the worst failure rate among the hours that carry volume at 17:00 — 39.92% on 1,062. Re-finding those proves you can follow instructions.

⚠️ Read This Twice

The grade is not for the technology

It is for the decision the data supports.

A simple dashboard with three clear charts and one sharp, quantified recommendation beats an elaborate one that ends at "here are some charts". Every time. Without exception.

Sharding, change streams, a custom React front end, a vector index — none of these earn marks on their own. They earn marks only when they are the reason you could answer a question you otherwise could not.

The test

Finish this sentence: "We built this so that the business could decide ______." If you can't, cut it and spend the time on the recommendation.

65 of 100 marks go to framing, modelling and insight. Only 25 to technical execution. Optimise accordingly.
What Good Sounds Like

A recommendation in four sentences

✓ Specific, costed, owned, testable

"Among the hours that carry real volume, driver-side failure is worst at 17:00 — 39.92% on 1,062 bookings, against the 18:00 peak's 37.14% on 1,373 — worth an estimated ₹13.3 lakh a year. We recommend a ₹40 surge incentive on 16:00–18:00 airport pickups, capped at 300 rides a day. If it converts a third of those cancellations it recovers about ₹4.4 lakh against ₹1.3 lakh of incentive spend. We would run it four weeks in one zone first."

✗ The version that scores 6/15

"Cancellations are a significant issue, particularly in the evening. The business should investigate driver behaviour during peak hours and consider appropriate interventions."

The difference is not analysis. Both teams found 17:00. The difference is a number attached to an action — and a denominator attached to the number.

Avoid These Six

Where previous cohorts lost marks

FailureIn the roomFix
No recommendationTwelve minutes of charts, then "thank you"Write the recommendation slide first. If you can't draft it, you don't have a project
Vanity metricsA ticking total nobody can act onIf this number doubled tomorrow, who does what differently? Nobody → cut the chart
Unlabelled axesy-axis to 4,000 — rides? rupees? minutes?Units on every axis; denominator beside every percentage: "17.99% (2,699 of 15,000)"
Rate with no volume floor"Our worst hour is 03:00 at 43.48%" — on 46 bookingsApply a volume floor before ranking; show the denominator beside every rate
Causation from correlation"Failures are worst at 17:00, so drivers avoid the evening"Say "associated with", name what you can't rule out, propose the test
A borrowed modelNew data forced into the rides shape; sub-documents nobody queriesRe-derive from your access pattern
Demo with no fallbackFour minutes watching a spinnerScreenshots in the deck + a recording. Switch in 15 seconds
None of these are technical failures. All seven are judgement failures.
Worked Example

Rank by rate alone and you chase noise

HourFailure rateBookingsWhat it actually is
03:0043.48%46Noise — two more failures moves this four points
07:0041.60%637Suggestive, still thin for a costed intervention
16:0040.45%764The shoulder of the real problem
17:0039.92%1,062The finding — worst rate among hours carrying volume
18:0037.14%1,373Busiest hour of the day — lower rate on more rides

✗ The claim that collapses under questioning

"03:00 is our worst hour." Forty-six bookings. No intervention can reach them.

✓ The claim that survives

"The worst failure rate among hours that carry volume is 17:00 at 39.92% — above the 18:00 peak's 37.14%, though 18:00 takes 1,373 bookings to 17:00's 1,062."

{ $group:     { _id: { $hour: "$booking_datetime" }, bookings: { $sum: 1 }, failed: { $sum: ... } } },
{ $match:     { bookings: { $gte: 1000 } } },   // <-- the volume floor, stated on the slide
{ $addFields: { failure_rate: { $divide: ["$failed", "$bookings"] } } },
{ $sort:      { failure_rate: -1 } }
Choose the floor deliberately and declare it. A floor you can defend shows you understand your numbers; one you can't is as bad as none.
Ground Rules · Today

The timer is visible and absolute

⏱ 12:00 hard stop

Warning at 11:00. Stop at 12:00 — mid-sentence if necessary. Content not reached is content not marked.

🎤 5 minutes Q&A

Questions go to the whole team. The chair may direct one at a named member. Everyone needs the model and the headline numbers.

🔌 Set up in advance

The next team connects and tests during the previous team's Q&A. Changeover is not slot time.

📝 Forms filled live

Score during the presentation, not from memory at the end. No interruptions during the twelve minutes — write the question down.

Every board paper and client readout you will ever give has a hard stop. People who overrun are people who did not decide what mattered.

Peer Evaluation

Score every team, 1–5, on three things

DimensionThe question you are answeringScore 1Score 5
Clarity of the problemDid you know, within 90 seconds, whose problem this was and why it mattered?Still unclear at the endObvious immediately, never lost
Credibility of the analysisDo you believe the numbers, and would they survive a sceptical question?Unsupported or inconsistentSourced, denominated, defended
Strength of the recommendationCould a manager act on this on Monday?Vague or absentSpecific, costed, owned, testable

Peer scores inform the mark — they do not set it

The examiner marks against the rubric; peer data corroborates. Scoring your friends uniformly 5 helps nobody and is visible in the distribution.

You are evaluating against the same rubric you are marked by. That is the point of the exercise.
Demo Hygiene

Four rules, all learned the hard way

  1. Pre-load the data and warm the cluster. M0 clusters idle down. Run the dashboard ten minutes before your slot so your demo's first query isn't the one paying the cold-start cost.
  2. Carry screenshots and a recording. Every demo view as an image in the deck, plus a two-minute walkthrough video. Rehearse the switch: fifteen seconds and one sentence.
  3. Do not rely on venue wi-fi. Assume forty devices are on it. Bring a hotspot you have actually tested by loading your dashboard over it.
  4. Know your own numbers cold. Source, denominator, exclusions — for every figure on every slide.

⚠️ No credentials on screen

No connection strings, passwords or API keys on a slide, in a screenshot, or in a public repo. Blur the host in Atlas captures. Credentials shown to forty people get rotated that evening.

"I'd have to check" about your own headline metric is the most damaging answer available in Q&A.
Wrap-Up

Six things to carry into the room

  1. The grade is for the decision, not the technology. 65 of 100 marks sit in framing, modelling and insight.
  2. Five requirements are non-negotiable — loaded data, a non-trivial query, index evidence or a key schema, a dashboard, a written model justification.
  3. Bring your own dataset. Re-finding 62.29% proves you follow instructions; finding something new proves you can do the work.
  4. Twelve minutes is a hard stop. Decide what matters before the room decides for you.
  5. Most marks are lost to judgement, not code. No recommendation, vanity metrics, unlabelled axes, a rate with no volume floor, causal overreach, a borrowed model, no fallback.
  6. Score peers honestly, live, on clarity, credibility and strength of recommendation.

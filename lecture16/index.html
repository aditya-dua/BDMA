Interpretation 1

Causes really are evenly spread — so any single fix addresses at most a quarter. Say so before someone budgets for a silver bullet.

Interpretation 2 — more likely

Three of four blame the customer. Drivers pick from a dropdown under time pressure; the lowest-penalty option wins. This is a measurement artefact.

“Do not design an intervention around these codes until they are validated” is a real, defensible recommendation.

Stress Test · 1 & 2

Attack your own finding first

“Is this a small sample?”

Fair — it is exactly what catches 03:00 (43.48% on 46 bookings). But 17:00 carries 1,062. Stable to a fraction of a point.

Survives.

“Isn't 39.92% ≈ 37.14%?”

2.78 points — a 7.5% relative increase. About 30 extra failed trips in that hour, ~₹7,000 of fares. Material over a year, modest in one hour.

Partially survives — target a window, not a point.

Do this in private, before someone does it to you in a board meeting.
Stress Test · 3 & 4

The two attacks most analysts never run

“Composition, not time?”

If 17:00 carries more airport trips, and airport trips fail more, the hour is innocent. Segment 17:00 by vehicle type and pickup, compare to the daily mix. Same mix + different rate ⇒ time is the driver.

“What would make this wrong?”

If driver log-ins showed the same active supply at 16:00–17:00 as at 18:00, repositioning collapses. We do not have that data — and saying so names exactly which dataset resolves it.

This is the check that separates a finding from a coincidence — and the rubric rewards it.

The Boring Chart

A flat distribution is a finding when it rules something out

Cancellation rates across the top five hubs span 31.24% to 33.50% — barely two points. There is no catastrophic location.

What that proves

The problem is systemic, not local. A fix aimed at “the bad hub” cannot work, because there is no bad hub.

What it buys you

The intervention must be time-based and system-wide, concentrated where volume already is. The chart that looked boring did real work.

Combined: target 16:00–18:00 at the five hubs carrying 39% of demand. Defined, costable, measurable.

Layout

Readers scan in a Z

Top-left — read first

The stake. The status donut frames everything that follows.

Top-right — read second

The mechanism. The 16:00–18:00 failure curve.

Bottom-left

Supporting detail — revenue by product, cancellation reasons.

Bottom-right — read last, ignored first

Never put your key finding here.

Three rules do most of the work: title states question + population · every axis carries its unit · every number traces to a runnable pipeline.
The Deliverable · 1 of 2

Ride Completion — Recommendation

Finding

5,657 of 15,000 bookings (37.71%) carried no passenger, against ₹22.0 lakh realised. At ₹235.73 average fare that is up to ₹13.3 lakh forgone — an upper bound, since some customers rebooked.

Diagnosis

At least 24.89% of all bookings (3,734) fail on the supply side. A further 419 customer cancellations cite “driver asked to cancel” or “driver not moving”, pushing the share toward 27.6%.

Mechanism

Failure peaks at 16:00 (40.45%) and 17:00 (39.92%), above the 18:00 peak's 37.14% despite 18:00 carrying more bookings. Supply lags demand by one to two hours.

The memo is the deliverable. The dashboard is the appendix.
The Deliverable · 2 of 2

…and what we will do about it

Recommendation

Pilot a time-boxed driver incentive, 16:00–18:00, at the five highest-volume hubs (39% of demand). Size it below ₹235.73 so the economics hold at any recovery rate above break-even.

Expected impact

Closing the 16:00–17:00 gap to the 18:00 baseline recovers ~60 trips/day across the two hours — roughly ₹14,000 daily, ₹51 lakh annualised, before incentive cost.

How we will know

Compare pilot vs non-pilot hubs over three weeks. If the gap does not close by at least one percentage point, the explanation is wrong and we stop.

Naming the condition under which you abandon your own recommendation is the strongest credibility signal available.
Why It Works

Five properties of a page that gets acted on

  • Every number has a denominator. Not “cancellations are high” but “3,734 of 15,000”.
  • The recommendation has a verb and a boundary. Pilot — these five hubs — this window — below this price.
  • The impact is arithmetic the reader can check. Show the multiplication; never assert the product.
  • There is a stopping rule. A date and a threshold at which you walk away.
  • The limitations are specific. “We lack driver log-in data” is useful. “Further research is needed” is filler.
A reader should be able to act, or refuse to act, in ninety seconds.
Limitations

Under-reporting limits is what destroys trust

GapWeakStrong
No driver supply data“More data would help.”“Driver log-in timestamps would confirm or kill this in a day.”
Unreliable reason codes“Reasons are unclear.”“Three of four blame the customer in near-equal shares — consistent with penalty-avoidance.”
₹13.3 lakh estimate“Approximate.”“Upper bound assuming no rebooking. The conclusion holds even if it is half.”
Correlation only“Causation unproven.”“The pilot is the experiment. That is why we test on a subset.”
Pattern: name the gap, state what closes it, say whether the conclusion survives. Handled that way, a limitation adds authority.
